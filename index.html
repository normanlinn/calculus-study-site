Q1 · §2.8Use the limit definition to find \(f'(x)\) for \(f(x)=x^2+3x\).
Hint

Expand \(f(x+h)=(x+h)^2+3(x+h)\). After subtracting \(f(x)\), every term left has an \(h\).

Step-by-step solution

\(f(x+h)=x^2+2xh+h^2+3x+3h\)

\(f(x+h)-f(x)=2xh+h^2+3h=h(2x+h+3)\)

\(\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}=2x+h+3\xrightarrow{h\to0}2x+3\)

\(f'(x)=2x+3\)

Q2 · §2.8Find the slope of the tangent line to \(f(x)=x^2-4x\) at \(x=3\).
Step-by-step solution

The tangent slope is \(f'(3)\). \(f'(x)=2x-4\) (by the definition, or the power rule).

\(f'(3)=6-4=2\)

Q3 · §2.8At what value is \(f(x)=|x-2|\) not differentiable? Explain.
Step-by-step solution

For \(x\gt 2\), \(f(x)=x-2\) has slope \(+1\). For \(x\lt 2\), \(f(x)=-(x-2)\) has slope \(-1\).

At \(x=2\) the one-sided slopes disagree, so the graph has a corner and \(\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(2+h)-f(2)}{h}=\lim\frac{|h|}{h}\) does not exist.

Not differentiable at \(x=2\) (corner: left slope −1, right slope +1). It is still continuous there. See Lab 2 with “|x|”.

§2.8

The Derivative as a Function

The slope of the tangent line at every point, collected into a brand-new function.

In §2.7 you found the slope at one point \(a\). Now let the point move. For every \(x\), the slope of the tangent line to \(y=f(x)\) is a number, so the slopes themselves form a function, written \(f'\):

\[ f'(x) = \lim_{h\to 0} \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} \]

The fraction is the slope of a secant line through two points on the curve, a horizontal distance \(h\) apart. As \(h\to 0\), the second point slides into the first and the secant becomes the tangent. Try it in the first lab.

Secant → tangent

Watch the secant slope approach f′(a) as h shrinks, from either side (h > 0 or h < 0). That limit is the derivative.

Reading f′ off the graph of f

Exercises 1–14 ask you to sketch \(f'\) from a picture of \(f\). You never need a formula. Just ask, at each \(x\): how steep is \(f\), and is it going up or down?

  • Where \(f\) has a peak or valley (horizontal tangent), \(f'=0\): the \(f'\) graph crosses the x-axis.
  • Where \(f\) is increasing, \(f'>0\) (above the axis). Where \(f\) is decreasing, \(f'\lt 0\) (below).
  • Where \(f\) is steepest, \(|f'|\) is largest.
  • Where \(f\) is a straight line, \(f'\) is a constant (a flat segment).

When is f not differentiable?

Corner

Left slope ≠ right slope. Example: \(|x|\) at 0 (slopes −1 and +1).

Discontinuity

A jump or hole. Theorem 4: differentiable ⇒ continuous, so not continuous ⇒ not differentiable.

Vertical tangent

The slope blows up: \(|f'(x)|\to\infty\). Example: \(\sqrt[3]{x}\) at 0.

Careful

Continuous does not imply differentiable. \(|x|\) is continuous at 0 but has a corner.

Notation and higher derivatives

Same thing, many names

\(f'(x) = y' = \dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{df}{dx} = \dfrac{d}{dx}f(x) = Df(x)\)

Second derivative

\(f''=(f')'\), \(\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}\). If \(s(t)\) is position: \(v=s'\), \(a=v'=s''\), jerk \(j=s'''\).

Domain of f′

\(f'\) can have a smaller domain than \(f\). \(\sqrt{x}\): domain \([0,\infty)\), but \(f'(x)=\frac1{2\sqrt x}\) only on \((0,\infty)\).

Worked examples

Example · definition with a square root (Stewart Ex. 3 style)

Find \(f'(x)\) for \(f(x)=\sqrt{x}\) using the definition. State the domain of \(f'\).

  1. Write the difference quotient: \(\dfrac{\sqrt{x+h}-\sqrt{x}}{h}\).
  2. Plugging in \(h=0\) gives \(\frac00\). For square roots, multiply by the conjugate.
    \(\dfrac{\sqrt{x+h}-\sqrt{x}}{h}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt{x}}{\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt{x}} = \dfrac{(x+h)-x}{h(\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt{x})}\)
  3. The \(x\)'s cancel, leaving \(\dfrac{h}{h(\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt x)} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt x}\). The \(h\) cancels.
  4. Now let \(h\to0\): \(f'(x)=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt x}\).
  5. Domain of \(f'\): \((0,\infty)\). At \(x=0\) we would divide by zero (vertical tangent).
Example · definition with a fraction (Stewart Ex. 4 style)

Find \(f'(x)\) for \(f(x)=\dfrac{1-x}{2+x}\).

  1. \(\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = \dfrac{1}{h}\left[\dfrac{1-x-h}{2+x+h}-\dfrac{1-x}{2+x}\right]\)
  2. Fractions: combine over a common denominator. Numerator: \((1-x-h)(2+x)-(1-x)(2+x+h)\).
  3. Expand: \((2 - x - 2h - x^2 - xh) - (2 - x + h - x^2 - xh) = -3h\).
  4. So the quotient is \(\dfrac{-3h}{h(2+x+h)(2+x)} = \dfrac{-3}{(2+x+h)(2+x)}\).
  5. Let \(h\to0\): \(f'(x) = \dfrac{-3}{(2+x)^2}\). Domain: \(x\neq-2\).

§2.8 #21Use the definition to find \(f'(x)\) for \(f(x)=3x-8\). State the domains of \(f\) and \(f'\).
Hint

\(f(x+h)=3(x+h)-8\). Subtract \(f(x)\) and see what survives.

§2.8 #23Use the definition to find \(f'(t)\) for \(f(t)=2.5t^2+6t\).
Hint

Expand \((t+h)^2=t^2+2th+h^2\). After subtracting, every surviving term has an \(h\) you can factor out.

Full solution

\(f(t+h)=2.5(t^2+2th+h^2)+6t+6h = 2.5t^2+5th+2.5h^2+6t+6h\)

\(f(t+h)-f(t)=5th+2.5h^2+6h = h(5t+2.5h+6)\)

Divide by \(h\): \(5t+2.5h+6\). Let \(h\to0\).

\(f'(t)=5t+6\). Domains: all real numbers.

§2.8 #28Use the definition to find \(F'(v)\) for \(F(v)=\dfrac{v}{v+2}\). State domains.
Hint

Common denominator \((v+h+2)(v+2)\). The numerator collapses to a multiple of \(h\).

Full solution

\(\dfrac{v+h}{v+h+2}-\dfrac{v}{v+2} = \dfrac{(v+h)(v+2)-v(v+h+2)}{(v+h+2)(v+2)}\)

Numerator: \(v^2+2v+hv+2h - v^2-vh-2v = 2h\).

Quotient: \(\dfrac{2h}{h(v+h+2)(v+2)}=\dfrac{2}{(v+h+2)(v+2)}\xrightarrow{h\to0}\dfrac{2}{(v+2)^2}\).

\(F'(v)=\dfrac{2}{(v+2)^2}\). Domain of \(F\) and \(F'\): \(v\neq-2\).

§2.8 #29Use the definition to find \(g'(u)\) for \(g(u)=\dfrac{u+1}{4u-1}\).
Hint

Same fraction strategy. After expanding, \(u^2\) and \(u\) terms cancel and you get \(-5h\).

Full solution

Numerator of the combined fraction: \((u+h+1)(4u-1)-(u+1)(4u+4h-1)\).

\(=(4u^2-u+4uh-h+4u-1)-(4u^2+4uh-u+4u+4h-1) = -5h\).

So \(\dfrac{g(u+h)-g(u)}{h}=\dfrac{-5}{(4u+4h-1)(4u-1)}\to\dfrac{-5}{(4u-1)^2}\).

\(g'(u)=\dfrac{-5}{(4u-1)^2}\), domain \(u\neq\tfrac14\).

§2.8 #31Use the definition to find \(f'(x)\) for \(f(x)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+x}}\). State domains.
Hint

Combine the fractions first: \(\dfrac{\sqrt{1+x}-\sqrt{1+x+h}}{\sqrt{1+x+h}\,\sqrt{1+x}}\). Then multiply the top and bottom by the conjugate \(\sqrt{1+x}+\sqrt{1+x+h}\).

Full solution

\(\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}=\dfrac{\sqrt{1+x}-\sqrt{1+x+h}}{h\sqrt{1+x+h}\sqrt{1+x}}\)

Multiply by the conjugate. The numerator becomes \((1+x)-(1+x+h)=-h\):

\(=\dfrac{-h}{h\sqrt{1+x+h}\sqrt{1+x}\,(\sqrt{1+x}+\sqrt{1+x+h})}\)

Cancel \(h\), let \(h\to0\): \(\dfrac{-1}{\sqrt{1+x}\sqrt{1+x}\cdot2\sqrt{1+x}}=\dfrac{-1}{2(1+x)^{3/2}}\).

\(f'(x)=-\dfrac{1}{2(1+x)^{3/2}}\). Domain of \(f\) and \(f'\): \((-1,\infty)\).

§2.8 #33(c)For \(f(x)=1+\sqrt{x+3}\), use the definition to find \(f'(x)\). What are the domains of \(f\) and \(f'\)?
Hint

The 1 cancels immediately. What remains is the \(\sqrt{\ }\) conjugate trick.

Full solution

\(\dfrac{\sqrt{x+h+3}-\sqrt{x+3}}{h}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt{x+h+3}+\sqrt{x+3}}{\sqrt{x+h+3}+\sqrt{x+3}}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x+h+3}+\sqrt{x+3}}\)

\(f'(x)=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x+3}}\). Domain of \(f\): \([-3,\infty)\). Domain of \(f'\): \((-3,\infty)\).

§2.8 #30Use the definition to show \(\dfrac{d}{dx}x^4=4x^3\).
Hint

\((x+h)^4=x^4+4x^3h+6x^2h^2+4xh^3+h^4\) (Pascal's row 1 4 6 4 1).

Full solution

\(\dfrac{(x+h)^4-x^4}{h}=\dfrac{4x^3h+6x^2h^2+4xh^3+h^4}{h}=4x^3+6x^2h+4xh^2+h^3\)

As \(h\to0\), every term with \(h\) vanishes: \(f'(x)=4x^3\). This is the Power Rule you'll use in 3.1.

§2.8 · higher derivativesIf \(f(x)=x^3-x\), find \(f'\), \(f''\), \(f'''\), \(f^{(4)}\).
Full solution

\(f'=3x^2-1\), \(f''=6x\), \(f'''=6\), \(f^{(4)}=0\).

Each derivative of a polynomial lowers the degree by one, so a cubic hits 0 at the 4th derivative.

Traps

Plugging in h = 0 too early

You always get 0/0 at first. Simplify until the \(h\) in the denominator cancels, then set \(h=0\).

Forgetting to distribute the minus

\(f(x+h)-f(x)\): put \(f(x)\) in brackets. Most definition errors are sign errors here.

Domain of f′ = domain of f

Not always. Check for division by zero in \(f'\) (e.g. \(\sqrt{x}\) at 0).

§3.1

Derivatives of Polynomials and Exponential Functions

The shortcuts that replace the limit definition.

Constant\[\frac{d}{dx}(c)=0\]

A horizontal line has slope 0.

Power Rule (any real n)\[\frac{d}{dx}x^n = n\,x^{n-1}\]

Bring the power down, subtract one.

Constant multiple\[\frac{d}{dx}[c\,f]=c\,f'\]
Sum / Difference\[(f\pm g)'=f'\pm g'\]
Natural exponential\[\frac{d}{dx}e^x=e^x\]

e is the one base whose slope at 0 is exactly 1.

Rewrite before you differentiate. The Power Rule needs the form \(x^n\). Convert roots and fractions first:

\[\sqrt{x}=x^{1/2},\quad \sqrt[3]{x^2}=x^{2/3},\quad \frac{1}{x^3}=x^{-3},\quad \frac{4}{\sqrt[3]{x}}=4x^{-1/3}\]

If a fraction has a single term on the bottom, split it: \(\dfrac{3x^2+x^3}{x}=3x+x^2\). No quotient rule needed.

Why e is special

f(x) = aˣa = 2

so f′(x) = (that slope) · aˣ–

For any base, the derivative of aˣ is aˣ times a constant (its slope at 0). When a = e that constant is exactly 1, and the pink derivative curve lands right on top of the blue curve.

Tangent and normal lines

The tangent line at \((a,f(a))\) is \(y-f(a)=f'(a)(x-a)\). The normal line is perpendicular to the tangent, so its slope is \(-\dfrac{1}{f'(a)}\).

A horizontal tangent happens where \(f'(x)=0\). Set the derivative to zero and solve.

Worked examples

Example · rewrite, then differentiate

Differentiate \(y=\dfrac{\sqrt{x}+x}{x^2}\).

  1. Split: \(y=\dfrac{x^{1/2}}{x^2}+\dfrac{x}{x^2}=x^{-3/2}+x^{-1}\).
  2. Power rule on each: \(y'=-\tfrac32x^{-5/2}-x^{-2}\).
  3. Optional cleanup: \(y'=-\dfrac{3}{2x^{5/2}}-\dfrac1{x^2}\).
Example · horizontal tangents (Stewart Ex. 7)

Find the points on \(y=x^4-6x^2+4\) where the tangent line is horizontal.

  1. \(y'=4x^3-12x=4x(x^2-3)\).
  2. Set \(y'=0\): \(x=0\) or \(x=\pm\sqrt3\).
  3. Plug back into \(y\) (not \(y'\)): \(y(0)=4\), \(y(\pm\sqrt3)=9-18+4=-5\).
  4. Points: \((0,4)\), \((\sqrt3,-5)\), \((-\sqrt3,-5)\).

Traps

Power rule on eˣ

\(\frac{d}{dx}e^x\neq xe^{x-1}\). The power rule is for variable base, constant exponent.

Negative exponents

§3.2

The Product and Quotient Rules

The derivative of a product is not the product of the derivatives.

Product Rule\[(fg)' = f\,g' + g\,f'\]

“first · d(second) + second · d(first)”

Quotient Rule\[\left(\frac fg\right)' = \frac{g\,f' - f\,g'}{g^2}\]

“low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared”. The order matters because of the minus.

The product rule is an area picture

Δ(uv) / Δx–
u v′ + v u′ = x·2x + x²·1–

The area uv grows by two strips plus a tiny corner: Δ(uv) = u·Δv + v·Δu + Δu·Δv. Divide by Δx and let Δx → 0. The corner is a product of two small numbers, so it disappears, leaving (uv)′ = u v′ + v u′. Drag Δx toward 0 and watch the two bottom numbers meet.

Worked examples

Example · product rule with eˣ

§3.3

Derivatives of Trigonometric Functions

Six formulas, two limits, and a cycle of four.

sin and cos\[(\sin x)'=\cos x\]\[(\cos x)'=-\sin x\]
tan and cot\[(\tan x)'=\sec^2x\]\[(\cot x)'=-\csc^2x\]
sec and csc\[(\sec x)'=\sec x\tan x\]\[(\csc x)'=-\csc x\cot x\]
Memory trick

Every “co-” function (cos, cot, csc) gets a minus sign. tan ↔ sec² and cot ↔ csc² pair up; sec and csc reproduce themselves.

The slope of sin x is cos x

The two special limits

Limit 1\[\lim_{\theta\to0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}=1\]

θ must be in radians. The argument of sin must match the denominator.

Limit 2\[\lim_{\theta\to0}\frac{\cos\theta-1}{\theta}=0\]

Why sin θ / θ → 1

Lab 5b · shrink the angle
arc length θ–
vertical side sin θ–
sin θ / θ–

On a unit circle the arc has length θ and the pink segment has length sin θ. As θ shrinks, the arc straightens out and the two lengths become equal, so their ratio → 1.

Using the limit: make the denominator match the angle. Multiply and divide by what you need:

\[\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin 7x}{4x}=\frac74\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin7x}{7x}=\frac74\cdot1=\frac74\]

Higher derivatives of sin cycle every 4: \(\sin\to\cos\to-\sin\to-\cos\to\sin\). To find \(\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}\sin x\), divide \(n\) by 4 and use the remainder.

§3.4

The Chain Rule

Derivatives of functions inside functions. Rates multiply.

Chain Rule\[\big(f(g(x))\big)'=f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)\]

Derivative of the outside (leave the inside alone) × derivative of the inside.

Leibniz form\[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}\]

If u changes 3× as fast as x, and y changes 2× as fast as u, y changes 6× as fast as x.

Power + chain\[\frac{d}{dx}[g(x)]^n=n[g(x)]^{n-1}g'(x)\]
Exponential + chain\[\frac{d}{dx}e^{g(x)}=e^{g(x)}g'(x)\]\[\frac{d}{dx}a^x=a^x\ln a\]

A nudge travels down the chain

Lab 6 · y = sin(x²)
Δu / Δx ≈ du/dx = 2x–
Δy / Δu ≈ dy/du = cos u–
product of the two–
exact dy/dx = 2x·cos(x²)–

The inner function u = x² stretches the nudge by a factor of 2x. The outer function y = sin u stretches it again by cos u. Total stretch = the product. That is the chain rule.

How to spot inside vs. outside

Ask: if I plugged in a number, what would I compute last? That's the outer function.

  • \(\sqrt{x^3+1}\): last step is the square root. Outer \(\sqrt u\), inner \(u=x^3+1\).
  • \(\sin^2x=(\sin x)^2\): last step is squaring. Outer \(u^2\), inner \(\sin x\).
  • \(\sin(x^2)\): last step is sin. Outer \(\sin u\), inner \(x^2\).
  • Long chains peel like an onion: \(\big(f(g(h(x)))\big)'=f'(g(h(x)))\cdot g'(h(x))\cdot h'(x)\).

§3.5

Implicit Differentiation

Finding slopes on curves you can't (or don't want to) solve for y.

A curve like \(x^3+y^3=6xy\) isn't a function \(y=f(x)\), but near most points it looks like one. So treat \(y\) as an unknown function of \(x\) and differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\).

The one rule: every time you differentiate something with \(y\) in it, the chain rule attaches a factor of \(y'\). So \(\frac{d}{dx}(y^3)=3y^2\,y'\), \(\frac{d}{dx}(\sin y)=\cos y\,y'\), and \(\frac{d}{dx}(xy)=y+x\,y'\) (product rule).

Recipe:

  1. Differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\). Attach \(y'\) to every \(y\)-term.
  2. Move all \(y'\) terms to one side, everything else to the other.
  3. Factor out \(y'\) and divide.

Tangent lines on implicit curves

point (x, y)–
dy/dx formula–
slope here–

When the denominator of dy/dx is 0, the tangent is vertical. When the numerator is 0, it is horizontal.

Worked examples

Example · folium of Descartes (Stewart Ex. 2)

Find \(y'\) for \(x^3+y^3=6xy\) and the tangent line at \((3,3)\).

  1. Differentiate: \(3x^2+3y^2y'=6y+6xy'\). Right side used the product rule on \(6xy\).
  2. Collect \(y'\): \(3y^2y'-6xy'=6y-3x^2\).
  3. Factor and divide (and cancel 3): \(y'=\dfrac{2y-x^2}{y^2-2x}\).
  4. At \((3,3)\): \(y'=\dfrac{6-9}{9-6}=-1\). Tangent: \(y-3=-(x-3)\Rightarrow x+y=6\).
Example · second derivative (§3.5 #39)

Find \(y''\) for \(x^2+4y^2=4\).

  1. \(2x+8yy'=0\Rightarrow y'=-\dfrac{x}{4y}\).
  2. Quotient rule on \(y'\), remembering \(y\) is a function: \(y''=-\dfrac{4y\cdot1-x\cdot4y'}{16y^2}\).
  3. Substitute \(y'=-\frac{x}{4y}\): \(y''=-\dfrac{4y+\frac{x^2}{y}}{16y^2}=-\dfrac{4y^2+x^2}{16y^3}\).
  4. Use the original equation: \(x^2+4y^2=4\). \(y''=-\dfrac{4}{16y^3}=-\dfrac{1}{4y^3}\).

9. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(\csc x\right)\) =

“co-” function → minus sign.

10. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\,2^x\) =

\((a^x)'=a^x\ln a\). Only base e has no extra factor.

One-page cheat sheet

Everything you need to memorize, in one place.

Definition (2.8)

\[f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\]

Tangent line: \(y-f(a)=f'(a)(x-a)\). Normal slope: \(-1/f'(a)\).

Basic rules (3.1)

\[(x^n)'=nx^{n-1},\ \ (e^x)'=e^x,\ \ (c)'=0\]

\((cf)'=cf'\), \((f\pm g)'=f'\pm g'\)

Product / Quotient (3.2)

\[(fg)'=fg'+gf'\]\[\left(\tfrac fg\right)'=\tfrac{gf'-fg'}{g^2}\]

Trig (3.3)

\[\begin{aligned}(\sin x)'&=\cos x & (\cos x)'&=-\sin x\\(\tan x)'&=\sec^2x & (\cot x)'&=-\csc^2x\\(\sec x)'&=\sec x\tan x & (\csc x)'&=-\csc x\cot x\end{aligned}\]

\(\lim\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}=1\), \(\lim\frac{\cos\theta-1}{\theta}=0\)

Chain (3.4)

\[\big(f(g(x))\big)'=f'(g(x))\,g'(x)\]

\((g^n)'=ng^{n-1}g'\), \((e^{g})'=e^{g}g'\), \((a^x)'=a^x\ln a\)

Implicit (3.5)

Differentiate both sides; every \(y\)-term gets a \(y'\); collect, factor, solve.

\((y^n)'=ny^{n-1}y'\), \((xy)'=y+xy'\)
