Maximum and Minimum Values

Critical numbers, and the Closed Interval Method for absolute extremes.

0 / 20 problems mastered

This section is about finding the highest and lowest values of a function. It starts the “applications of derivatives” chapter, and every optimization problem later builds on it.

Absolute vs local

Absolute max/min: the highest/lowest value on the whole domain or interval. Local: highest/lowest compared with nearby points only.

Extreme Value Theorem

If fff is continuous on a closed interval [a,b][a,b][a,b], it has an absolute max and an absolute min there.

Fermat's Theorem

If fff has a local max or min at ccc and f′(c)f'(c)f′(c) exists, then f′(c)=0f'(c)=0f′(c)=0.

Critical number

A number ccc in the domain of fff where f′(c)=0f'(c)=0f′(c)=0 or f′(c)f'(c)f′(c) does not exist.

The Closed Interval Method

To find the absolute max and min of a continuous fff on [a,b][a,b][a,b]:

  1. Find the critical numbers of fff that lie inside (a,b)(a,b)(a,b).
  2. Evaluate fff at each of those critical numbers.
  3. Evaluate fff at the endpoints aaa and bbb.
  4. The largest value is the absolute max; the smallest is the absolute min.

Traps

Forgetting the endpoints

On a closed interval, the absolute max or min is often at an endpoint (#55: the max is at x=−2x=-2x=−2).

Critical numbers outside the interval

Only test critical numbers that lie inside [a,b][a,b][a,b].

Points not in the domain

Where f′f'f′ is undefined only counts if fff itself is defined there (#37: x=12x=\frac12x=21​ doesn't count).

f′ = 0 means extreme

Not always: x3x^3x3 has f′(0)=0f'(0)=0f′(0)=0 and no extreme there.
